Neurphology: a neuronal morphology analysis algorithm

An ImageJ macro that measures somas and neurites in fluorescence microscopy images, and how it compared with manual tracing on 20 images.

B.Sc. thesis in biotechnology · 2009 · Chalmers University of Technology and National Chiao Tung University

Summary

The thesis describes Neurphology, an ImageJ macro that detects somas and neurites in images of cultured neurons and reports counts and lengths. The aim was to replace the slow part of neuronal image analysis, where a researcher traces neurites by hand, which can take hours per image. It was tested on 20 images of TUJ1-stained P19 cells and compared with manual soma counting and manual neurite tracing (NeuronJ).

Context

The work was done over five months at the Lab of Cytoskeleton and Morphogenesis, Brain Research Centre, National Chiao Tung University, Hsinchu, Taiwan, with Dr Eric Hwang as advisor. According to the preface, developing the software was a request from the lab staff to ease their workload. The name Neurphology and the ImageJ-based approach are documented in this thesis.

Method

The pre-phase finds low-contrast pixels by shifting the image one pixel and subtracting, and low-intensity pixels from the image mode, and removes both. Somas are found with a morphological open filter and a threshold, and counted and sized. Removing the somas leaves the neurites, which are thresholded, skeletonized and analysed with the AnalyzeSkeleton plugin. The macro uses the ImageJ plugins Short Morphology, Particle Remover and AnalyzeSkeleton.

Test

Twenty images from cultures of TUJ1-stained P19 cells were analysed by hand and then by the software. Images 1–10 are NEUROD2-differentiated and images 11–20 MASH1-differentiated, which gives more aggregated neurites. Manual work took one week for five people. Neurite length was traced with NeuronJ, and somas were counted by eye.

Computed from this table: soma counts agree closely (correlation 0.96, mean absolute difference 7.6 %). Neurite length from the software is higher than the manual value in almost every image (mean ratio 1.34), by about 16 % in images 1–10 and about 52 % in the denser images 11–20, although the two methods rank the images in the same order (correlation 0.93). The thesis explains the gap by the software detecting contrast differences that are invisible to the human eye, and by manual tracing being very hard in dense meshworks. The thesis does not include an independent ground truth, so the data do not show which value is closer to the real length.

The thesis also estimates a maximum error rate of 2 % for the merged-image detection, based on counting missed particles over repeated runs.

Limits

  • The macro is semi-automatic. The user measures the soma width, sets thresholds by eye and types in endpoint counts, so results depend on those choices.
  • Endpoint counts are not accurate, because somas are removed before the skeleton is analysed. They are only used to calculate attachment points and neurites per soma.
  • Strongly aggregated somas and neurites remain hard to separate, and the software can count several touching somas as one (for example image 11).
  • Only 2D images, one cell type, one lab and 20 images.

Transcription notes

The per-image pages of the thesis contain a few values that look like typing errors: neurite length 500 000 for image 9 (Appendix 1 says 50 000), 32 500 attachment points for image 13, and 7 endpoints for image 15. The text also gives image 11 as 250 somas counted manually, where the Appendix 1 table says 240. The table above uses the Appendix 1 values.
